*I wrote this over a year ago to submit to the Atlantic op ed. They didn't take me up on it but if they're reading - I am open to negotiations.*

Feeling aimless in my work, down to my last few hundred dollars, living in a new country for the first time, watching from across the ocean the throttling of American scientists by the federal government, today, is when I learned of Dickson Despommier's passing, and cried on the Danish commuter bus back to my apartment.

I never met Dickson Despommier. However, in 2014 I took my first biology class at Virginia Western Community College in Roanoke, Virginia. The acute need for education—and the stability and wealth it ostensibly would bring—was deeply impressed on me for perhaps the first time that year. My late arrival to this realization I can partly attribute to being hard-headed in high school. For example – my 10th grade English teacher called me her "biggest disappointment"— of her 30-year teaching career. She retired that year. At the time I took this as a badge of honor, having prided myself in being an artist. Obsessed with literature and music, I felt, and still often feel, something passionate in me, a weight of emotion, profundity, that needs to be expressed. When I was a teenager, this manifested in a rebellious head-strong streak that did not translate to a strong academic work ethic.

When I started community college we had just lost our family home. I had packed up my green 1994 Honda and drove down to Salem, VA to live with my father in my grandmother's house, who was in hospice care nearby. She had bought the house in 1954 working as the head cashier at the local Kroger and now it was on lien due to her persistent medical expenses and debt. Friendly, unknowing families would periodically tour the house, threatening to leave us homeless at a moment's notice. Motivated to succeed (or rather scared to fail) in my first elective science course, I searched for podcasts and other online resources to draw from. By chance or luck, this led me to the successful and expansive *This Week in Virology*, a podcast run by prominent virologist Vincent Racaniello and co-hosted by Dickson Despommier, both of Columbia University.

I now know there are many different ways to think about scientists as individuals. There are explorers, optimizers, mechanics, among many other varieties. Dickson was an artist scientist. Dickson spoke loudly and brashly and occasionally out of turn, but always from the heart and the mind. His co-hosts, colleagues, and friends recount his love of talking and teaching; the eagerness and kindness with which he spoke and how he inspired others from all walks. It seems to me that he expressed through his actions that science is not disconnected from people, but rather it both is and is for people — and that we cannot separate the discipline of science from its human core. The revelation of the artist-scientist was a paradigm shift for me. Over the course of that semester, I realized for the first time that I could cultivate within myself science as a form of art, a way to do "homage to the beauty of the world" and, I would add, an homage to the beauty of people. In just a couple of years, I enrolled at Virginia Tech to study biochemistry and commuted an hour and twenty minutes every day to attend. My research experience grew, including trips to Flint, Michigan as part of a team investigating *Legionella pneumophila* in the area associated with an outbreak of Legionnaire's Disease, and eventually a PhD.

Many scientists have been thinking about visibility lately, including me. In particular, I recently found an old anthology of poems, and the section introducing Gertrude Stein quoted: "The world will ask who you are, and if you do not know, the world will tell you." It resonates. I think about it especially as I observe the plurality of people—including many of my friends and family—on social media cheering on the federal government's dealings with institutions of higher education. On March 10th, the official NIH X-account tweeted out its announcement that Colombia University will be losing more than 250-million dollars of NIH funding "following directives from the Trump Administration's Joint Task Force to Combat Anti-Semitism. The cancellations are due to Columbia University's continued inaction in the face of persistent harassment of Jewish students" in response to campus policy on student protests. The funding cuts include millions of dollars for cancer and diabetes research and will directly impact the lives of thousands of graduate students, research scientists, and other faculty. In subsequent days, ICE apprehended Mahmoud Khalil, a graduate student at Columbia and a legal resident of the United States, who was linked to campus protests. Whether this cut remains in effect, is a singular event, or reflects the future for years to come, it is painful to watch the system I love—flawed though it may be—under assault. Fueled by coffee and a sincere desire to be useful to the world, there are millions of scientists in the US who work hard to support a society that increasingly does not understand them. Like others, I feel compelled to say that current events are an urgent call to action for scientists to express themselves and who they are to whomever will listen, which is why I am writing to you today.

Dickson taught me a lot about who I am, though I never met him. I know I am not the only one. At the close of the *This Week in Parasitology* episode dedicated to Dickson, Dr. Racaniello recounts his experience watching Dickson fly fish on Bitterroot River in Montana. Catching fish after fish with apparently the perfect lures and the exact right wrist movements, he astonished the river boat guide, while kissing each fish before releasing them back into the water.
